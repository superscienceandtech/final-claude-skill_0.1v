## Inside a certified quality system

Where the plant holds a certification — ISO 9001, IATF 16949, AS9100 or a customer-specific scheme —
the quality system stops being purely internal. It carries obligations that are audited, and the
audit tests the system rather than the product.

What that changes in practice:

- **Nonconformance has to be dispositioned and recorded**, not just fixed. Use as-is, rework, repair,
  scrap, return — each with an authority level that says who may decide, and the reasoning attached.
  An undocumented "we sorted it" is the finding.
- **Corrective action is a defined loop with evidence at the end.** Containment first, so nothing
  further escapes while the cause is being found; then cause; then the action; then verification that
  the rate actually moved; then closure. Auditors open closed actions and look for the last step,
  because it is the one most often missing.
- **Document and change control is part of the product.** Which revision of the drawing, the work
  instruction and the inspection plan was in force when the part was made is a question that will be
  asked, sometimes years later.
- **Traceability runs backwards from the customer.** Given a complaint, the system has to identify
  the lot, the material, the machine, the shift and everything else made under the same conditions.
  That defines the recall boundary, and a system that cannot draw it makes the boundary everything.

**Certification is evidence of a system, not of quality.** A plant can hold a clean certificate and
ship defects; the certificate says the process is documented and followed. Treat an audit finding as
information about the system and the escaped-defect rate as information about the product, and do
not let a clean audit answer a customer complaint.

Customer-specific requirements sit on top of the standard and usually carry the sharper teeth —
production part approval, annual revalidation, mandated containment on escape. Know which customer
imposes what before the escape, not after.
