## Inbound materials behave differently from inbound goods

Everything above holds for a supply chain that moves finished goods. A plant's inbound side has
properties that change the answers.

**Lead time variability costs more than lead time.** A material that reliably takes six weeks can be
planned. One that takes two to six weeks cannot, and the difference is absorbed as either safety
stock or missed production. When pressing a supplier, press on the variance before the average.

**A shortage of any one input is a shortage of the finished item.** Ninety-nine of a hundred
components in stock is zero units buildable. This is why component-level availability, not aggregate
inventory value, is the number that predicts whether the schedule holds — and why inventory
reduction programs measured in dollars reliably delete the cheap part that stops the line.

**Qualification makes second-sourcing slow.** Where material is specified, certified, or bound to a
customer approval, an alternate supplier is a qualification project measured in months, not a
purchase order. The time to start is before the single source is a problem. Rank single-sourced
inputs by what stopping costs per day and qualify downward from the top.

**Minimum order quantities and batch sizes propagate.** A supplier minimum that exceeds a run's
requirement pushes the excess into inventory or forces a longer run than the schedule wanted. That
is a supply decision reshaping the production schedule, and it should be visible as one rather than
absorbed at the plant.

Scrap and yield sit between the two sides: material consumed is what was issued, not what shipped,
and a plan built on finished quantity under-orders by the yield gap every time.
